01 · The axis you described

Given Order ↔ Self-Authorship

Kept from v1, but demoted. It is no longer the measuring instrument — it's the hypothesis the emergent factors will be tested against.

Given Order Self-Authorship
A Man for All Seasons The Lord of the Rings The Lion King Gladiator Frozen Maleficent Dead Poets Society

Where a story locates moral authority — in what is inherited, revealed, or written into the world; or in what the self and the excluded can rightfully claim.

Given Order

A right order precedes the protagonist. It is not chosen — it is inherited, revealed, or woven into the world: a Circle of Life, a rightful line, a natural law. Wrongdoing is deviation. The hero's flaw is refusal or exile, and the arc is submission. Simba does not invent a self; he remembers one. "Remember who you are" is the entire thesis in four words. Evil arrives as a usurper, and the ending is a restoration — the last shot is the first shot.

Self-Authorship

The inherited order is the problem: built by someone's ambition, kept at someone's expense. Authority sits with the self's own truth and with whoever the order excluded. Maleficent takes a story whose architecture was pure Given Order — christening, curse, prince, restoration — and re-reads every beam: the king is the violator, the witch is the wounded party, the prince's kiss simply fails. Aurora becomes whole by leaving her place. Nothing is restored.

02 · The method change

Discover the factors, don't declare them

You're right to refuse the hand-labelling, and not only because it's laborious. Scoring 60 films on eight axes I invented would have measured my ontology with your labour. If the eight axes are real, they should fall out of the films rather than be imposed on them.

There is a well-trodden path for exactly this. The Big Five personality factors were not designed by anyone — they fell out of factor analysis over thousands of trait adjectives people actually use. Nobody decided "openness" should exist; it was what remained standing when the correlations were rotated. The same move works here, with films as respondents and moral propositions as items.

First, what will not work

Embedding plot summaries and running PCA or UMAP on the vectors. This is the obvious move and it fails in a specific, predictable way: sentence embeddings encode what a film is about, not what it believes. Your PC1 will be period-drama-versus-science-fiction, PC2 will be war-versus-romance, and The Lion King will sit beside Madagascar because both are animated animal pictures. Moral stance is a thin signal riding on top of an enormous topical one, and unsupervised reduction will find the loud thing every time. The propositions below exist to strip the topic out before any geometry happens.

1

Extract a moral skeleton per film

Unchanged from v1 and still the substrate. From the plot synopsis: what the film treats as the source of legitimacy; the antagonist's nature and origin; whose interiority is granted; who holds power at open and close; who is punished, who forgiven; what the final image asserts. Verbatim evidence quotes throughout. This is topic-stripped by construction — it never mentions dragons or spaceships.

2

Generate the item bank from the films themselves

Take ~300 films spanning era, country and genre. From each skeleton, have the model write 10–20 moral propositions the film affirms or denies — free-form, in its own words. That yields ~4,000 raw statements. Cluster by embedding, merge near-duplicates, and cut to a bank of roughly 300. The vocabulary is now the corpus's, not mine.

3

Score every film against every item

For each film: affirms +, denies −, or doesn't address ·. Output sparsely — only the items the film actually engages, typically 40–80 of 300 — which is cheaper, more accurate, and gives you the salience mask for free. The bank is an identical prefix on every call, so cache it and pay ~10% for it.

4

Factor analyse the film × item matrix

Polychoric correlations (the data is ordinal and sparse, so Pearson will mislead), parallel analysis to choose the number of factors, oblimin rotation for interpretability. Unrotated components are almost never legible; rotated factors usually are. Then name each factor from its top-loading items — and because the items are readable English sentences, naming is honest rather than imaginative.

5

Test the emergent factors against the eight axes

Score the v1 axes too — they're cheap — and correlate. If emergent Factor 1 hits 0.8 with Order Verdict, that's convergent validity and you can name it with confidence. If nothing lines up, the eight axes were wrong and the data has told you so. Either result is worth having; only the imposed version couldn't fail.

04 · The labelling you can do

Three hours, no expertise required

Your instinct — mark the films you morally agree with and find the pattern — is the right supervision signal. It just needs a companion task, because agreement alone tells the model where you stand without telling it how the space is shaped.

The circularity trap

It will be tempting to have the system write you a moral brief for a film you haven't seen so you can label more of them. Don't — you would be labelling the model's reading, the model would then be validated against its own reading, and the agreement would be perfect and meaningless. Verdicts only on films you've watched. Eighty is plenty; the item matrix is doing the heavy lifting, and the labels only need to locate you within it.

05 · The dimension question

Can it be one axis? Yes — but not the one you'd get from PCA

Straight answer on the unsupervised side: expect four to seven interpretable factors, with the first explaining somewhere around 15–25% of variance. Moral space is genuinely not one-dimensional, and a plot that pretends otherwise will put films in places you can't defend. If PC1 came back at 60% I'd suspect an artefact rather than celebrate.

The supervised axis is one-dimensional by construction, and it's the one you actually want. Regress your 80 verdicts against the factor scores — regularised logistic regression, or LDA — and the resulting discriminant direction is your moral axis. Project all 2,000 films onto it and you have a single ribbon from "aligned with you" to "opposed to you", every position explainable by the items that drove it. That axis is honestly personal rather than falsely universal, which is also a better product: not the moral spectrum, but yours.

Then the two-dimensional map worth building is your alignment axis on x, and the largest factor orthogonal to it on y. Plotting alignment against something independent of alignment is what reveals variety within agreement — the difference between five films you'd endorse for five different reasons. Alignment against a correlate would just give you a diagonal smear.

06 · Validation without a gold set

Four ways to know it's real

Against you

Triplet satisfaction

What fraction of your 150 similarity judgements does the space reproduce? Above ~75% it has captured your moral perception; near 50% it has captured nothing. The headline number, and it needed no axis definitions from you.

Against itself

Split-half reliability

Split the item bank in two at random, factor each half separately, correlate the film scores. Unstable factors are noise wearing a name.

Against theory

Convergence with the eight

Do the emergent factors recover Order Verdict, Locus of Evil, Circle of Concern? Recovering some and refusing others is the most informative outcome available.

Against cases

The split-case battery

A fixed list of films that must not collapse together — Les Misérables apart from Maleficent, Joker apart from both, Hacksaw Ridge apart from Dead Poets Society. A regression test for the ontology; run it after every re-fit.

07 · Evidence

What each film needs

Your instinct that summaries aren't enough is right. The reason isn't quite the one you named — and the difference decides what you reach for instead.

The bias is real, but it's the wrong bias

Wikipedia's plot guideline demands neutral, in-universe event recounting with interpretation removed, and editors largely comply. So summaries don't editorialise much — they strip. What's gone is tone, point of view, irony, and which moments are given weight. A plot summary will tell you Simba returns and defeats Scar. It will not tell you the film treats that as the restoration of a sacred order rather than as a successful coup.

That's a worse problem than editor slant, and it points somewhere different: not at screenplays, but at whatever preserves framing — the film's own dialogue, and other people's readings of it.

Where editor bias does bite is narrower and partly measurable: which acts get three sentences and which get one, and causal verbs. "Maleficent, betrayed by Stefan, curses the child" and "the evil fairy curses the child" are different sentences, and only one of them is in the film.

Why subtitles rather than screenplays

The version problem settles it. Most circulating screenplays are shooting drafts or earlier — endings get rewritten, characters cut, the last scene replaced in the edit. Our propositions are settled disproportionately at the ending. A draft script is therefore wrong in precisely the place we care about most, and confidently so. Subtitles are transcribed from the finished film.

Coverage compounds it. Script archives skew hard toward prestige, English-language, post-1990 titles — the opposite of the spread factor analysis needs, and a corpus that shape will hand you factors that are really about era and prestige. Subtitle archives run to hundreds of thousands of titles across every decade and language.

And subtitles carry two things nothing else does. Timestamps give you act position for free — slice the closing 15% and extract the ending state with real confidence. And they give you the last line of spoken dialogue, which in a startling number of films is the thesis stated outright.

Two practical notes. Prefer SDH / closed-caption tracks: they carry speaker labels and non-dialogue cues that plain SRTs drop, and speaker attribution matters a great deal for whose interiority the film grants. And a subtitle track is ~10k tokens against a screenplay's ~25k, with far more of it load-bearing rather than slug lines and transitions.

Keep screenplays as an opportunistic fourth layer where they exist. Never as the backbone.

Packet · identity

Metadata

TMDB and IMDb ids, title, year, runtime, country, original language. Genres and keywords. Director, writers, and billed cast in order — billing position is a weak but free point-of-view signal. Franchise, collection, and every "based on" link. Budget and revenue.

These are also the covariates every confound check in section 05 regresses against, so none of it is optional.

Packet · text

Raw sources

Wikipedia plot section with its word count kept as a flag. Themes, Analysis and Reception sections. Three to five critic reviews. The SDH subtitle track, plus pre-cut opening-10% and closing-15% slices. Screenplay where one exists.

Not the TMDB overview. That is marketing copy, written specifically to conceal the ending — the worst possible input here, and a tempting fallback because it's always present.

Packet · derived

The skeleton

Power state at open and at close. Antagonist: is an origin given, and is their fate destruction, reconciliation, or escape. The protagonist's flaw and what replaces it. Whose interiority is granted. Who is punished, who forgiven, who dies. Final image and final spoken line. Tonal register with an irony flag. Source text and what it inverts.

Packet · audit

Provenance

Every proposition score records which layers supported it, and an evidence-completeness score rides on each film. This is what turns "are summaries good enough" from an argument into a query you can run — and stops thin-evidence films quietly distorting the factors.

Settle it with $10 rather than a week of arguing

Take forty films where all four layers exist. Score them three ways — spine only, subtitles only, everything — and measure per-proposition disagreement.

Under ~10%: summaries are sufficient, you skip 20M tokens of subtitle processing, and you bought that decision with evidence rather than assumption. Over ~25%: your concern was right, and you now know which propositions are summary-resistant — so you fetch subtitles for those rather than for everything.

Add one targeted probe for the bias you actually asked about: measure the Sleeping Beauty → Maleficent displacement vector from summaries and from subtitles separately. Those two summaries are written by different fandoms, so if editor slant is large anywhere it is largest there — and an inflated displacement is exactly what it would look like.

Let availability define the corpus

Your other agent finding full plots for only a modest subset is corpus design, not an obstacle. Don't choose 2,000 films and then hunt for their data — choose the 2,000 films that already have all four layers. Complete-evidence films are the well-documented, widely-seen ones, which is the same population your verdict labels can cover anyway. The constraint and the requirement point the same direction.

On posture: Wikipedia is CC BY-SA and the TMDB API is free with attribution — both clean. Subtitles and screenplays are not yours to redistribute. Store the derived skeleton and the item scores durably; treat raw subtitle and script text as a cache you can delete without losing work. That keeps the project clean if it ever leaves your machine.

Stack & costs

  • Model: claude-opus-5 for skeletons and item generation — the judgement is the product. Item scoring against a fixed bank is a more mechanical task; benchmark claude-haiku-4-5 against Opus on a 200-film overlap in phase 2 and drop down if agreement holds.
  • Batch API for both sweeps: half price, nothing latency-sensitive. Prompt caching on the item bank — a 300-sentence prefix identical across 2,000 calls is close to the ideal case, and it's most of the saving above.
  • Analysis: Python. factor_analyzer for polychoric FA and rotation, scikit-learn for the discriminant, DuckDB for the matrix. Version every score row with item_bank_version and run_id — you'll re-cut the bank at least twice.
  • Total ~$95 for the full instrument, and a re-sweep after changing the bank is ~$70. Cheap enough that the bank is a decision you can revisit rather than defend.
10 · Open

Three decisions

  • Corpus. TMDB top 2,000 by popularity, or seeded from your Letterboxd / IMDb history? Strong case for the second now: your verdict labels can only cover films you've seen, so a corpus weighted toward your viewing gives the discriminant far more to work with.
  • Language. Python throughout is the obvious call — the factor analysis has no real equivalent elsewhere. Front end can be a static page with Observable Plot unless you'd rather it were TypeScript end to end.
  • Phase 0 now? Forty films, about two dollars, and it answers the only question that matters before any of this is worth building: do the raw propositions read as moral claims or as plot paraphrase? Say the word and I'll build it.
